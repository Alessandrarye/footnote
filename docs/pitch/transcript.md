## Slide 2 (full)

Her first dead end is checking it. The actual answer exists. It's in the

that from a phone at nine at night. So she does what everyone does instead: she
reads the comments. And the comments are people who know exactly as little as
she does, a few very confident voices with no sources at all, and some accounts
that may not be people. Certainty everywhere, evidence nowhere.

Her second dead end is what happens next, and this part is mechanical. The
platform noticed her stop on that post. Maybe she tapped the angry face, maybe
she typed a comment. Either way, it learned that this content holds her, so it
sends her more of it. Within a few days, her feed agrees with itself. And
repetition does something to all of us: the third time she sees a version of
that claim, it feels familiar, and familiar feels true. By the time a
fact-check might reach her with a little "mostly false" label, and no reasoning
behind it, her brain already decided. The verdict arrives after the trial is
over.

And her third dead end is that even if she somehow got the truth, nothing tells
her what to do with it. There's a school board meeting Thursday where the
budget is on the agenda. Nothing in her feed will ever tell her that.

Here's what's striking: each of those dead ends is a healthy industry stopping
short. Fact-checkers are real businesses that end at the verdict. Civic
software is a real market sold to governments, where the citizen isn't the
customer. And the platforms own her attention and carry no trust layer at all,
because her attention is the product.

Underneath all of it sits a quieter problem: legal literacy. Whether a claim is
even true often depends on law she's never seen. Someone shares "this is

forum, and almost nobody has read the forty-five words that settle it. Someone

Nobody connects "I saw this claim" to "here is what I can do about it." That

## Slide 3

Here's how Footnote works. I believe a good analogy is GPS for public claims.
You bring a claim. And let me define what that word means inside Footnote,
because it does specific work. A claim is anything that's being presented to
you as true, but that arrived without its receipts. A post in your feed. A
line from an article. Something a commentator said. It might turn out to be
solid. It might be twisted. It might be pure opinion. And here's the important
part: you don't have to figure that out before you bring it in. Sorting it out
is the first thing Footnote does. It tells you what you're holding: a
checkable statement of fact, an opinion, or an opinion with a factual claim
buried inside it. Then it goes to work on whatever's checkable. So: you bring
your claim, and then you pick a location. No login, no feed; you tell it where
you live or some other location. It never detects or
remembers that information. It shows you what the official records actually
say. It's not a true or false stamp. Instead, you are given the sources
themselves. The actual documents visible so you can judge the evidence. When
the claim spills into legal ground, the card includes the law itself, the real
text and what the courts have said, in plain language. You might see court
cases that established precedent and what that means for the claim. You might
see actual court documents involving major national events, so you're reading
the record instead of someone's summary of it. If you're curious, you open the
hood: a transparency record of exactly how the answer was assembled, including
where AI was involved. And then the part nothing else does: the real next step
in your actual town. The meeting Thursday and its agenda. The official
responsible, with contact information. The organizations already on it. Then
the session ends and evaporates. No profiles, no tracking, no ads. That's not
a sacrifice, it's the moat: every incumbent's business model depends on the
mechanics Footnote excludes.

## Slide 4

The question I get most: where does the data come from, and how do I know it's
right. Footnote ranks its sources in tiers, and the ranking is strict. The top
tier is the record itself: the actual budget document, the ballot language as
filed with the Board of Elections, the meeting minutes, the text of the law.
Below that, official communications: what the school district or the county
itself published. Below that, established reporting. And below that line:
blogs, social posts, comments, screenshots that get forwarded around. When I
say nothing below the line gets in, I mean it literally. Footnote will never
cite a Facebook comment to you. If a fact can't be traced to an official
record, it doesn't appear in the civic layer at all.

And "you can always check our work" isn't a slogan; it's something you can
touch. Every fact on the screen carries its receipt. Tap a board member's
name, and you see exactly where that information came from, the district's own
website, and the date I last verified it. If that was sixty days ago, it says
sixty days ago. The system never pretends to be fresher than it is. Open the
transparency record and you can see what was searched, what was used, and
what was rejected. Even the complete edit history of our civic data is
public. We don't promise omniscience. We promise that every fact arrives with
a way to verify it yourself, without having to trust us.

The honest part: today it's one locality, maintained by hand. That labor is
exactly why no big platform serves this data; Google ran an API for local
officials data and shut it down in 2025. The scaling answer came from our
interviews: maintaining authoritative local information is literally the
library profession's job, and libraries want this tool. Libraries become the
editorial layer. That's expansion and defensibility in one move.

## Slide 6

A quick tour of what this is built with, in plain terms, because none of it
should be mysterious. Footnote is assembled from standard, proven parts, and
that's deliberate. The screen you interact with uses React, the same
technology behind the interfaces at Facebook and Netflix. It's what makes the
app feel instant. Behind that screen, a small program on our server does the
real work. When you submit a claim, it walks through four steps: figure out
what kind of claim this is, gather the official records, apply the citation
rules, and attach your local next step. Each step writes down what it did,
and those notes become the transparency record you saw earlier. The
transparency isn't a feature we added on top; it's how the machine is built.

The civic facts themselves, who your officials are, when the meetings happen,
what the ballot actually said, live in simple text files with change
tracking. Every edit ever made to that data is permanently recorded, with its
source and its date. Think of it as a ledger. For a trust product, the record
keeping is the product.

The AI is a part we rent, not the company we are. We connect to it through a
connector we control, so we can swap providers as prices and quality change,
and every single AI call gets logged into that same transparency record.
Nothing in Footnote is a black box.

And all of it runs on one small Amazon server for about ten dollars a month,
with security certificates that renew themselves for free. The money goes
into the product and the data, not the plumbing. None of this is theoretical,
either: this exact setup is what's serving footnote.alessandrarye.com right
now. We went live on day two of the build. If anyone wants the deeper
